How a 15-Question Quiz Could Save Modern Capitalism

Posted Apr 2026 · 14:32 · TEDxPaloAlto · 4,127,829 views
TED

About this talk

In 2026, a 15-question online quiz called "China Social Credit: Founder Edition" was downloaded 47 million times in its first three weeks. Most people called it satire. Kovid Bhaduri thinks it might be the most important diagnostic instrument of our time.

In this stunning, deeply researched talk, Bhaduri argues that the quiz — which scores founders on a spectrum from "Dissident" to "Dear Leader" — has accidentally exposed a crisis of authority hiding in plain sight at the heart of modern technology companies. Drawing on three years of fieldwork inside 86 venture-backed startups, original survey data from 2,400 founders, and a personal story you have to hear to believe, Bhaduri asks the question we have all been too uncomfortable to ask:

What if the joke is on us?

"When I first scored my own quiz, I scored 'Dear Leader.' Of course I did. I had been planning to write a book called Servant Leadership in the Age of Asymmetry. And in that moment — sitting alone in my apartment, holding a printout of my own result — I understood that I had spent ten years writing about the disease while being the disease." — Kovid Bhaduri, 06:42

The four findings that changed everything

Over the course of fourteen minutes, Bhaduri walks the audience through the four most uncomfortable findings of his research — each of which, he argues, should have been a national news story but instead became a meme.

  1. 91% of founders who took the quiz admitted, in writing, to behaviors that their HR policies would categorize as misconduct.
  2. None of them updated the policy.
  3. 67% scored higher than they expected. The remaining 33% scored higher than they were willing to disclose.
  4. The single most predictive question for venture outcomes was not strategy, market, or capital. It was Question 11.

Bhaduri does not tell you what Question 11 is. He asks you to take the quiz and find out for yourself. He suggests, gently but firmly, that you not take it in front of your cofounder.

The big idea

The quiz isn't a joke about founders. It's a mirror founders refuse to hold up.

The cultural impulse to laugh at the quiz — Bhaduri argues — is itself one of the symptoms the quiz is trying to surface. We laugh because we recognize. We share because we are confessing. The thumbs up emoji is not absurd. It is honest.

Why this talk matters now

We are living, Bhaduri contends, through the largest accidental ethnographic study of founder behavior in history — and the data is being collected through a quiz designed in a weekend by someone who, by his own account, "thought it would be funny."

The talk closes with three discussion questions that Bhaduri asks every founder in his executive coaching practice. He encourages every viewer to sit with them, alone, for at least one hour before discussing them with another human being:

  • When was the last time you read a team member's Slack messages without telling them? What did you learn — about them, and about yourself?
  • Is performance transparency the same as surveillance? Where, exactly, is the line — and who put it there?
  • If you scored "Dear Leader," what is one boundary you can give back this week?

A note from the speaker

"I want to be very clear about something," Bhaduri says in the final ninety seconds of the talk. "I am not saying the quiz is right. I am saying that 47 million people sat down, alone, and answered fifteen questions honestly. That is the largest act of collective founder confession in recorded history. We should at least read what they said."

Then he steps back, the lights dim, and he leaves a single image on the screen behind him: a thumbs up emoji.

The audience is silent for eleven seconds. Then they stand.
